Thinking from our shore

Thinking the Caribbean from its territories, struggles, and forms of life

We study Black lives, Afro-Caribbean philosophy, the geographies of power, popular cultures, practices of community care, and situated forms of critical thought in the Colombian Caribbean.

“Thinking from our shore does not mean speaking from the margin, but from a historical position capable of interrogating the center.”

Pensamiento Caribe starts from a conviction: the Caribbean is not a margin but a historical zone where modernity, racialization, violence, creation, and resistance intersect. Researching from the Caribbean means listening to wounded territories, bodies that remember, and forms of thought that have been systematically silenced.

The Caribbean as a unit

The Greater Caribbean

The coastal border that shapes the Caribbean Sea forms a geographic, historical, and political unit. The Colombian Caribbean is one shore of that larger whole: a constellation of territories joined by the sea.

Conceptual legend

This map is the territory that our logo inhabits: the region bounded by the Caribbean Sea. Each node is a port, city, or territory of the Greater Caribbean; the layers group regions by their place in the Caribbean unity: archipelagos, continental coasts, the Guianas, and the Gulf. The map draws an archipelagic way of thinking, with Édouard Glissant: islands and shores that relate without merging, a poetics of relation, over a historical sea, with Derek Walcott: «the sea is history», the sea as an archive of the slave trade, the crossings, and the forms of resistance. The Colombian Caribbean appears highlighted as the group's shore of enunciation. Click each point to see its legend: history, popular culture, figures, and keys to thought.

Interactive relational atlas Growing corpus

Con el agua de por medio: an atlas for an archipelagic anthropology

One hundred and fifty-four works and three hundred and thirteen relations organized by ten cross-cutting phenomena (coloniality, post-plantation, racial capitalism, marronage, creolization, body and eroticism, among others), not by national borders. Each relation between works is typed as resonance or dissonance and cites its own source, open to correction.

The atlas declares that half of its relations still have no verified source, and since July 2026 it has taken contributions from anyone who travels it: absent works, untraced links, unrecorded frictions, corroborations, and corrections. The conduit is called the returning tide, and it holds contributors to the same rule the corpus applies to itself.

Eleguá Research Seedbed

The Eleguá research seedbed aims to open paths for students of Anthropology and other programs to join the academic and research processes of the Pensamiento Caribe Research Group. Inspired by Eleguá as the figure who opens roads and possibilities, this space seeks to accompany the training of new generations of researchers committed to the realities, memories, and, above all, the experiences of the Black lives of the Colombian Caribbean.

The seedbed is made up of students, seedbed members, and graduates who, through a collective construction of knowledge, seek to strengthen their academic paths through diverse forms of research, creation, and expression. It fosters dialogue between anthropology and the arts, film and video, writing, orality, and other methodologies that allow the group to communicate and reflect on work carried out from and for the Colombian Caribbean.

The seedbed is conceived as a space of encounter, learning, and mutual accompaniment, where critical reading, theoretical discussion, research-creation, and the exchange of experiences contribute to strengthening academic projects and to developing situated perspectives on social dynamics.

Opening statement · October 12

Day of Interculturality and Antiracism

With resolution 0138 of May 31, 2021, Colombia's Ministry of Culture put an end to the ignominy of naming October 12 the Day of the Race, a date on which the "discovery" of America used to be celebrated by exalting the Hispanic heritage of the former colonies. While this is a significant step forward in shifting the meaning of the date, declaring October 12 the "Day of the Nation's Ethnic and Cultural Diversity" — very much in line with the multicultural foundations of our 1991 Constitution — we consider that this designation remains bound to a logic of white-mestizo supremacy that only celebrates ethnocultural diversity, based on principles of inclusion that do not question the structural and historical roots of inequality and ethnoracial injustice.

In 1914, in its imperial agony and nostalgia, Spain called this date the "Fiesta de la Raza" as a way of commemorating Columbus's great feat and his providential role in bringing the Christian faith and civilization to the "New World." With the end of the Second World War, the concept of race became a proscribed term, which is why "raza" was replaced by "hispanidad" and finally reduced, in 1987, to Spain's "National Day." As the Ministry of Culture itself states in its justification for the name change, most South American nations have renamed this date to honor the Indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples of Abya Yala and their contribution to the nations of the continent. And yet it is telling that we are the last to reframe this date around the banner of celebrating pluriculturality and inclusion. Without dismissing the major advances in legal recognition granted by the current Constitution, we consider that this date, rather than simply reaffirming what we already know — our cultural diversity — should place the emphasis on completing the unfinished process of decolonization: epistemological, institutional, and subjective decolonization.

For the Aymara and Quechua peoples, for example, 1492 marked the beginning of a pachakuty, an event of radical transformation of time and space that literally turned the world upside down. For the Puerto Rican philosopher Nelson Maldonado-Torres, it was a double catastrophe: Indigenous genocide and the enslavement of millions of Africans. In both cases it was a great trauma, a wound that does not close, kept open by the continuities of colonialism, manifest in the historical exclusion of racialized people within the criollo republics. Racism is inscribed in history. Which is to say, it has not always existed. The racism we suffer today has its origin in the Iberian colonization of what we now know as America, and what Andean Indigenous nations call Abya Yala. Racism thus carries a historical density that must be unraveled, since it is one of the most damaging legacies of colonialism as an organizer of anthropological differences that justify exclusion, super-exploitation, and subhumanization.

For this reason, we do not celebrate the nation's cultural diversity. What we do commemorate are the struggles that, from the arrival of the pale, armored men bearing the Cross and the sword, resisted colonization in countless ways. That is why, from the Colombian Caribbean, we declare October 12 the Day of Interculturality and Antiracism: a date that connects Indigenous and Black resistances and ontologies with the utopian horizon of an intercultural society free of racisms.

Podcast · Gaceta Sonora

El Club Negro

In 1943, Manuel Zapata Olivella and Natanael Díaz founded an Afro-Colombian intellectual and political movement that fought against the invisibility and discrimination of the Black population in Colombia. Academic community

Who we are

Pensamiento Caribe brings together researchers, young researchers, and seedbed members committed to critical, situated, and responsible research from the Colombian Caribbean.

We are a comparsa of thought and affect. Diverse singularities that find resonance in the Afro-Caribbean syncopation. In the comparsa, differences are not erased, but sound synchronizes them into a rhythm — a cadence that forecloses neither distinction nor individual improvisation. We are compas who come together to think and move from our vital shore.

Researchers

Roberto Almanza Hernández

Director · Researcher

Anthropologist, with a master's degree in Cultural Studies and a PhD in Latin American Studies. Associate professor at Universidad del Magdalena and director of the Pensamiento Caribe Research Group. His work links Caribbean anthropology, Afro-Caribbean philosophy, and critical race theory, with sustained attention to Black lives and antiracism in the Colombian Caribbean. Author of La orilla de Calibán (Caliban's Shore), winner of the 2023 Casa de las Américas Prize, a critical genealogy of twentieth-century Afro-Caribbean philosophy. He has led the Anti-racist and Decolonial Week and projects on social cartography and political memory with Afro-Caribbean communities of the Magdalena Grande.

Territorial bond

Community, territory, and care

Pensamiento Caribe understands research as a process of collective construction sustained through listening, responsibility, giving back, and caring for its relationships with the communities and territories of the Colombian Caribbean. Communities are not objects of study: they are interlocutors, creators of knowledge, and protagonists of their own histories.

Collaboration

Research built with communities, not about them.

Listening

Attention to territorial voices, memories, and knowledges.

Giving back

A commitment to returning results to those who take part.

Care

Practices of care as a form of resistance and collective sustenance.

Collective training

Shared learning between academia, territory, and community.

Situated knowledge

Producing knowledge from territories and their concrete realities.
