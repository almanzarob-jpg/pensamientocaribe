The Caribbean as a unit

The Greater Caribbean: a relation of shores

The coastal border that shapes the Caribbean Sea forms a geographic, historical, and political unit. The Colombian Caribbean is one shore of that larger whole: a constellation of territories joined by the sea.

Conceptual legend

This map is the territory that our logo inhabits: the region bounded by the Caribbean Sea. Each node is a port, city, or territory of the Greater Caribbean; the layers group regions by their place in the Caribbean unity: archipelagos, continental coasts, the Guianas, and the Gulf. The map draws an archipelagic way of thinking, with Édouard Glissant: islands and shores that relate without merging, a poetics of relation, over a historical sea, with Derek Walcott: «the sea is history», the sea as an archive of the slave trade, the crossings, and the forms of resistance. The Colombian Caribbean appears highlighted as the group's shore of enunciation. Click each point to see its legend: history, popular culture, figures, and keys to thought.

Where we think from

A Caribbean that thinks from its own relations

“Thinking from our shore does not mean speaking from the margin, but from a historical position capable of interrogating the center.”

Pensamiento Caribe starts from a conviction: the Caribbean is not a margin but a historical zone where modernity, racialization, violence, creation, and resistance intersect. Researching from the Caribbean means listening to wounded territories, bodies that remember, and forms of thought that have been systematically silenced.

The anthropology of the Caribbean we propose starts from a specific place: the Colombian Caribbean, a shore in tension between Colombia, national anthropology, and the Greater Caribbean.

Research

Research lines

Anthropology of the Caribbean

Studies the Caribbean as a historical, cultural, and political formation, attending to its territories, mobilities, memories, inequalities, and forms of life.

Explore this line →

Afro-Caribbean philosophy

Explores traditions of Black and Afro-Caribbean thought, their critiques of racial modernity, and their ways of imagining freedom, humanity, and world.

See research →

Critical race theory and antiracism

Analyzes the historical production of race, the contemporary forms of racism, and the intellectual, pedagogical, and political practices of antiracism.

Explore this line →

Black geographies and territory

Investigates the relations between racialization, space, territory, city, landscape, dispossession, and the production of Black places in the Colombian Caribbean.

Related publications →

Racial capitalism, the plantation, and Caribbean modernity

Examines the plantation, racialized labor, accumulation, extractivism, and the historical infrastructures that have shaped Caribbean modernity.

Explore this line →

Popular cultures and Afro-Caribbean expressions

Studies the expressive, festive, musical, bodily, narrative, and aesthetic forms through which Afro-Caribbean communities produce memory, belonging, social critique, and historical presence.

Ongoing Greater Caribbean Atlas · DOI

With Water in Between

Two hundred and three works and four hundred and twenty-nine relations organised by ten cross-cutting phenomena —coloniality, post-plantation, racial capitalism, marronage, creolisation— rather than by national borders.

Ongoing Black Atlantic Cartography · DOI

With the Sway of the Currents

An interactive map connecting the Greater Caribbean with Africa through music, Blackness, and Pan-Africanism. Twenty-six round-trip routes, from the gumbé that returned to Freetown to the champeta with which Palenque answered.

Blackness and Monumental Whiteness

On the asymmetry between Blackness and monumental whiteness: what the monument does when it becomes landscape, archive, and institution, and what happens when it changes material and becomes an algorithmic model.

Research made in relation

Fourteen researchers, the Eleguá Seedbed, and three allied networks: the CLACSO Working Group on antiracism and Afro-descendance in the Global South, GLEFAS, and Malunga. The work is done with communities, not about them: the social cartography workshops, the neighbourhood memories, and the sound archives come out of collective processes.
