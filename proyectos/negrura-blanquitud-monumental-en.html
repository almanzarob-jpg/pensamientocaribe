Afro-Caribbean philosophy Book in preparation

Blackness against monumental whiteness

Research on what the monument does: fixing a version of the world, turning it into landscape, and erasing the labor that raised it. And on what happens when that monument changes material.

Line
Afro-Caribbean philosophy
Status
In progress
Start
January 2026
Researcher
Roberto Almanza Hernández

There is a comfortable temptation when writing about race: to present Blackness and whiteness as two opposing, comparable positions, each with its own history and its own claim. This research begins by rejecting that symmetry. Blackness was produced as a position by a concrete operation of capture, appraisal, and dispossession, and does not precede that operation. Whiteness, by contrast, produced itself as that which needs no naming: the norm against which everything else is measured, without ever appearing as one position among others.

That is what the adjective monumental is meant to specify. A monument does not argue or ask permission: it occupies the plaza, organizes traffic, and makes its version of the past look like the natural state of things. Whiteness operates this way when it becomes curriculum, canon, urban architecture, criterion of merit. And what the monument makes visible is exactly what it makes invisible: the labor that raised it.

If monumentality is an operation and not a material, it must be possible to recognize it on supports that are not made of stone.

That is where the contemporary part of the work is at stake. The systems that today classify, recommend, and surveil inherit the white norm and automate it, with an advantage the statue never had: they present themselves as pure calculation, with no place of enunciation and no history. Meanwhile, the labeling and moderation that feed them are done in the Global South, badly paid and unsigned. The structure is old and the medium is new: whoever builds remains the one left outside what is built.

The research thinks from Santa Marta and the Colombian Caribbean, not as a case to which a theory is applied but as the shore from which it is enunciated. It works with the Caribbean philosophical tradition and with contemporary Black theory, and it holds the discussion between them open rather than resolving it. Six categories have emerged from that work, and they are what organize the book. At the bottom of this page is the shape the whole framework is taking: a constellation of concepts and of the currents that link them.

The research will be published first as a book. That is why this page presents the argument and not its full development: the complete conceptual framework, with definitions and bibliography, will appear with the book. To discuss the project, write to the research group.
